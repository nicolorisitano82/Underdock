Widgets inside the Dock.
Yours, not a replica.

Almost every app in this category hides the system Dock and draws a fake one. This one doesn't: the widgets live in the real Dock, which keeps doing the layout, the magnification and the auto-hide. We only bring the content.

And a second place to put them: a panel that comes down from the notch, with a strip of actions under it — one of which opens a mirror that takes a photograph using the screen as its flash.

Three layers, no fake Dock

The Dock is a protected system process: you cannot inject code into it. But macOS leaves two doors open, and both of them are public API.

1

A plug-in inside the tile

NSDockTilePlugIn has existed since 2009 and nobody uses it any more. The Dock loads the bundle into an XPC service reserved for third-party extras and keeps it alive even when the app is closed. That is where the square tile is drawn.

2

An overlay on the spacers

A wide widget needs room, so we ask the Dock for it by inserting spacer tiles, read back over Accessibility where it put them, and rest a panel on top. The layout stays the Dock's — the magnification too.

3

A manager in the menu bar

No icon in the Dock: the space in the Dock belongs to the widgets. From the menu bar you turn them on, configure them, and on quit the Dock goes back to exactly what it was.

Eleven widgets, two shapes

Every widget is either a square tile or a bar as wide as you like — and several are both, switchable from one control. The images below are not mock-ups: they are the same views the Dock draws, rendered by a tool that compiles the project's own code. Four arrived after these were rendered: Notes, which shows the note you touched last and opens it; Shelf, somewhere to put a file down for a minute — drag it on, drag it back off, nothing moved and nothing copied; Appointments, reading Apple Calendar or a Google calendar's own secret iCal address; and Weather, from Open-Meteo, which answers without a key and without an account.

Clock — nine faces

Analog, digital, split-flap, concentric rings, minimal, the time spelled out the way you would say it, binary, a bar for how far into the day you are, and a station board that drops its digits. Seconds and date optional, and any time zone — which is how you keep a second city on the Dock.

Now Playing — interactive

Real cover art, a progress bar you can click to seek, controls that answer the pointer, and lyrics that scroll with the track — from LRCLIB, which answers without a key. As a tile it is a square with the cover and the progress.

Actions — four cells

An icon, a cell colour, an icon colour, and one action each: open an app, an address, a shortcut, or one of the system actions. No labels.

Sensors — one or many

CPU, memory, disk, network, battery, watts drawn, thermal state. As a tile, one sensor; as a bar, one per slot — and narrowing the bar never forgets what you had chosen.

Disks — every volume

Used space per volume, the boot disk first and external drives right behind it, appearing the moment you plug one in. Click opens it in the Finder, right-click ejects it.

Folder — alive

What is in it, counted, with the latest arrivals peeking out. Click and a glass sheet opens on the folder — rows that scroll, sorting, a chevron to go back — while the Dock holds still underneath. Drop a file on the tile and the same list comes up in copy mode, with a plus on every folder, so it lands in that one or in one of its children. The icon takes a colour and a symbol, the way the Finder customises a folder in macOS 26.

Note — in sight

A couple of lines you keep where you can read them. One click opens a panel above the bar to write in, and it saves the moment you look away.

A second place: the notch

Move the pointer onto the notch and a black panel comes down, 400 points wide. The same widgets, in a copy with settings of its own — and in the bar shape only, since that is the shape the panel has room for. At the top it flares outward where it meets the menu bar, the way the real notch does.

18°

Four slots: Now Playing has taken two of them, Weather and Appointments one each — and under the lot, the strip of actions.

Four slots

Every widget says how many it takes: one, or two where the second has something to say. Now Playing at two rows gives you a big cover, the controls and the lyrics; Weather opens the hours ahead; Appointments goes from the next thing to the whole day; Sensors lines the readings up instead of taking turns; Shelf shows what is on it rather than counting it. A widget grown to two pushes the last one off the list rather than overflowing the panel.

Action strip

Under the last widget, a thin line of up to ten small icons, white on black, centred: open an app, an address, a shortcut, one of the system actions — and the mirror.

Mirror

A borderless window showing what the camera sees, centred and flipped, the way somebody looking at themselves expects. Pinch to zoom towards the pointer, the wheel pans, a double click puts it back. The shutter takes a photograph to the Desktop, using the screen as the flash: white across the whole display with the brightness pushed up, and the frame taken once the white is actually there. Along the bottom sit the controls for the macOS ring light: on or off, five colours from amber to blue, and how much light. The controls are nearly invisible at rest and come up under the pointer, because the picture is the thing worth looking at.

Levels

In the black either side of the notch, brightness and volume. They belong to the panel: there while it is open, gone with it, so the menu bar stays the menu bar.

Field notes

Building inside the Dock means measuring instead of assuming. These are the things that are in no documentation, found by getting them wrong first.

MediaRemote

Since macOS 15.4 the now-playing registry only answers processes the system trusts. Same code, same instant: 32 keys inside the Dock's process, zero inside ours. So the plug-in became the privileged reader that relays the data to everything else.

Spacers

The Dock keeps keys it does not recognise inside its entries — until it rewrites that entry itself, which it does whenever it saves its own state. A first test said the mark survived; it had only survived one restart. So a spacer is ours by position, sitting right after the widget's tile, and the mark is only a hint.

Preferences

Writing and then sending killall Dock is a race: on its way out the Dock saves the copy it holds in memory, undoing your change. The sequence that works is suspend, write, kill while suspended. Four runs out of four.

Proportions

A macOS icon's art fills 824 points out of 1024; the rest is the margin its shadow lives in. Drawing 880 is enough to make a widget look bigger than its neighbours without anyone being able to say why.

Energy

powermetrics requires root. IOReport does not — but across 11,414 channels on this machine the only counter that moves is the GPU's. So every domain is probed, and one that stays silent is not even offered in the menus.

Ring light

The colour can be written and read straight back, and nothing on screen changes: the light only looks at its settings when the amount of light moves. Switching it off and on does not do it, with a pause between the two or without. So a colour change nudges the intensity by a fortieth and puts it back.

Gestures

Clicks are routed by where they land; gestures go to the key window. A borderless panel of an accessory application is never key, so the pinch was being delivered to whatever app happened to be in front — the buttons worked and the zoom did nothing.

Tile canvas

The Dock hands a tile plug-in a canvas of 128 points at scale 0. Magnified, that is drawn at twice the size — which is the whole of why a folder icon looked soft, and why the canvas is now 256.

Accessibility

The Dock reports its tile positions already magnified, and one read costs 0.04 ms. Following the magnification at 60 Hz costs 2.4 ms of CPU per second — which is the reason the overlay can exist at all.

It's open, and built to be extended

MIT licensed: take it, change it, sell it, no permission needed. The architecture is arranged so that a new widget is a file, not an expedition.

Adding a widget

  1. Draw it. A TileView for the square shape, a BarContentView for the wide one. Every measurement is a fraction of the tile.
  2. Declare it. One entry in WidgetCatalog: name, symbol, and — if it is a bar — its minimum width.
  3. Configure it. A PaneViewController for the settings pane.
  4. Build. build.sh assembles bundles, plug-ins, icons and signature on its own.

Get it, or build it

The disk image is signed with a local certificate and not notarised by Apple — notarising takes a paid developer account, which this project deliberately does not require. macOS will say it cannot check it: right-click the app, then Open, once. Building it yourself avoids that entirely.

All you need is swiftc from the Command Line Tools. No Xcode, nothing to download, no developer account. 

git clone https://github.com/nicolorisitano82/Underdock.git
cd Underdock
./Tools/make-signing-cert.sh   # once, so the permissions stick
./build.sh && ./install.sh

The self-signed certificate is not a flourish: macOS ties a permission to the binary's signature, and with an ad-hoc one every rebuild would quietly make you grant Accessibility all over again.
